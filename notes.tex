\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The two box-scale vortices which breathe on the zonal condensate of a strongly driven dipole pair plasma are the shear-flow instability of their own jets: in the fluid limit, jets whose shear layers are corrugated on the Debye scale are unstable in the sense of Rayleigh, and the unstable mode on the measured jets has the frequency and the radial structure of the vortices; it saturates by trapping. The vortices are damped kinetically: the field equation, with a metric that varies along the magnetic field, forces on them a small non-flute part, which is Landau damped by the particles streaming along the field, at a rate we calculate with no adjustable parameter and which is the measured one. The resonant particles carry the momentum of the damped vortices into the jets, so the jets pay for most of the loss, and the vortices drain by the small difference of two nearly cancelling exchanges with the jets, through the Reynolds stress and through the damping. The drain is slow; the collapse of the vortices in the reference simulation is set by its radial hyperdiffusion and grid.
\end{abstract}


% ===========================================================================
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Strongly driven electron--positron plasmas in a dipole collapse, after a single explosive burst of interchange activity, into a box-scale zonal condensate, i.e., a flow that does not vary in the direction of the flow itself and holds nearly all of the energy, on which two coherent vortices ride, one on each jet, carried in opposite directions and exchanging electrostatic energy with the zonal flow at twice their advection frequency --- the `breathing' of \citet{KennedyPlunkLetter}. Nothing in the collisionless transit-averaged model damps the flute-like zonal part of this state (flute-like meaning constant along the magnetic field line) \citep{KennedyPlunkManuscript, KennedyPlunkLetter}, and the net injection of free energy by the gradients is negligible; the condensate is therefore long-lived, and the transport it carries is close to zero. The vortices, however, are not protected in the same way: in a reduced-box simulation of the same state, their energy falls slowly for several thousand time units, then ever faster, until they collapse into a weak, partly incoherent remnant. Coherent structures riding on sheared flows are common in zonally dominated plasma turbulence \citep{Ivanov2020, Ivanov2025}, and their lifetime can be as important as their existence. Since the vortices carry the large oscillating fluxes of the condensate, what removes them --- and whether it would also remove them in a less idealised plasma --- determines the long-time state of the system.

The vortices are the shear-flow instability of their own jets. The zonal flow of the condensate is not a single harmonic: its shear layers are corrugated, on a scale of a few Debye lengths, and such jets are unstable, in the sense of \citet{Rayleigh1880}, to perturbations of the scale of the box. The instability saturates when the fluid at its critical layers, viz., the surfaces on which the flow moves with the perturbation, is trapped in `cat's eyes' of closed streamlines \citep{ONeil1965, Stewartson1978, WarnWarn1978}; the saturated state is the pair of breathing vortices, which, in turn, maintain the corrugation of their jets. This state is not lossless, however. A vortex is flute-like, i.e., constant along the magnetic field line, only at scales larger than the Debye length; the small part of its potential that varies along the field line does work on the particles that stream along it, and this Landau damping \citep{Landau1946} converts the electrostatic energy of the vortex into fine structure in velocity space, at a rate that does not depend on its amplitude. The jets make up for the loss, since the vortices draw on them, but only as long as the jets remain unstable, and the corrugation that makes them so is itself slowly eroded. The vortices and the corrugation of their jets therefore decay together, and the vortices collapse when the jets become stable. This picture is sketched in \cref{fig:cartoon}.

The damping mechanism belongs to a known family. A mode that is two-dimensional to leading order can acquire structure along the magnetic field from the geometry of the confinement, and particles that bounce or transit along the field then damp it at their orbit resonances: the rotational pumping of the diocotron mode of a non-neutral plasma, where the structure comes from the ends of the trap \citep{CrooksONeil1995}, bounce-harmonic Landau damping \citep{BerkBook1969, Anderegg2016}, and the collisionless damping of the geodesic acoustic mode of a tokamak \citep{SugamaWatanabe2006GAM}. What is particular here is the origin of the structure: in a plasma that is not quasineutral at the scale of the vortices, Poisson's equation with a metric that varies along the field line forces a non-flute part of relative size \(k^2\lambdaD^2\) on any vortex, and the vortices on which it acts are self-organised, in jets that Doppler-shift the resonance. That the particles absorb the momentum of the wave together with its energy, and that in a magnetic field the momentum becomes a radial current that drives the mean flow, is likewise known, in the theory of waves and mean flows \citep{AndrewsMcIntyre1976, Buhler2014}, in the momentum theorems for zonal flows \citep{Diamond2008}, in wave-driven transport in fusion plasmas \citep{FischRax1992} and in the damping of diocotron and Kelvin modes \citep{CrooksONeil1995, Kabantsev2014}; its consequence here, that the damping takes energy from the jets and gives part of it to the vortices, is what keeps them alive.

In this paper, we first summarise the setting in \cref{sec:setting}, viz., the transit-averaged equations, the strongly driven condensate and the reduced-box simulation with its restarts. In \cref{sec:theory}, we derive the theory: the fluid limit and the instability of the jets (\cref{sec:euler,sec:rayleigh}), its saturation by trapping (\cref{sec:pendulum}), the kinetic loss (\cref{sec:loss}) and the decay that results from the three together (\cref{sec:decay}). In \cref{sec:data}, we test each of these against the simulation. \Cref{sec:summary} summarises what is established and what is open.

\section{The setting}\label{sec:setting}
% ===========================================================================

In this section, we recall the equations of \citet{KennedyPlunkLetter} and \citet{KennedyPlunkManuscript} to the extent needed here, describe the strongly driven condensate in which the vortices live, and specify the simulations analysed in \cref{sec:data}.

\subsection{Transit-averaged equations}\label{sec:equations}

We consider a symmetric electron--positron plasma, with charges \(e_p = -e_e = e\), a common Maxwellian \(f_0\) of density \(n_0\) and temperature \(T\), in the ordering \(\rho \ll \lambdaD \ll L\), where \(\rho\) is the Larmor radius, \(\lambdaD = \sqrt{\epsilon_0 T/2n_0e^2}\) the Debye length and \(L\) an equilibrium length. We use flux coordinates \((\psi, \alpha, l)\) with \(\vB = \grad\psi\times\grad\alpha\), and write \(\delta f_a = h_a - e_a\phi f_0/T\). Below the transit and bounce frequencies, \(h_a(\psi,\alpha,\varepsilon,\lambda,t)\) is independent of \(l\) \citep{Helander2014, MPH2018}, where \(\varepsilon = mv^2/2\) is the energy and \(\lambda = v_\perp^2/Bv^2\) the pitch-angle variable, and particles respond to the orbit-averaged potential
\begin{equation}
\phibar(\lambda) = \frac{1}{\hat\tau(\lambda)}\int_{l_1}^{l_2}\frac{\phi(l)\,\rmd l}{\sqrt{1-\lambda B(l)}},
\qquad
\hat\tau(\lambda) = \int_{l_1}^{l_2}\frac{\rmd l}{\sqrt{1-\lambda B(l)}},
\label{eq:orbit}
\end{equation}
where \(l_1\) and \(l_2\) are the bounce points of a trapped particle or the ends of one field-line period for a passing one. To leading order in \(\kperp\rho\), the kinetic and field equations are \citep{Abel2013, MPH2018, KennedyPlunkManuscript}
\begin{align}
\pt h_a + \pbra{\phibar}{h_a} + \vda\,\partial_\alpha h_a
&= \frac{e_af_0}{T}\left(\pt\phibar - v_{*a}^{T}\,\partial_\alpha\phibar\right),\label{eq:kinetic}\\
\left(1-\lambdaD^2\nabla_\perp^2\right)\frac{e\phi}{T}
&= \frac{1}{2n_0}\int\rmd^3\vv\,(h_p-h_e),\label{eq:poisson}
\end{align}
where \(\pbrainline{X}{Y} \equiv \partial_\psi X\,\partial_\alpha Y - \partial_\alpha X\,\partial_\psi Y\) is the \exb{} advection, \(\vda\) is the orbit-averaged binormal magnetic drift, which is energy dependent and of opposite sign for the two species, and \(v_{*a}^{T} = -(T/e_a)\,\partial_\psi\ln f_0\) is the gradient drive at fixed energy. The dipole has no radial magnetic drift, so the zonal flow suffers no collisionless damping.

The nonlinearity of \cref{eq:kinetic} conserves the free energy \(W\) and the electrostatic energy \(E\) separately, and their difference \(Z = W - E \geq 0\) is the entropy of the orbit-averaged perturbation \citep{KennedyPlunkManuscript, KennedyPlunkLetter}; these invariants are the closed-field-line counterparts of those of two-dimensional gyrokinetics \citep{Plunk2010, PlunkTatsuno2011}. Here, we need only two further facts. Firstly, the free energy obeys
\begin{equation}
\frac{\rmd W}{\rmd t} = P - D,
\label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
where \(P\) is the injection by the gradients and \(D \geq 0\) is the irreversible removal, which, in the simulations, is entirely due to hyperdiffusion \citep{KrommesHu1994, Sugama1996}. Secondly, if we split \(E = \Ezon + \Enz\) into its zonal (\(k_y = 0\)) and non-zonal parts, the nonlinear transfer between the two satisfies
\begin{equation}
\left(\pt\Ezon\right)_\text{NL} = -\left(\pt\Enz\right)_\text{NL},
\label{eq:exchange}
\end{equation}
i.e., the zonal flow and the vortices exchange electrostatic energy without loss; this exchange is the breathing \citep{KennedyPlunkLetter}. We shall write \(\Znz\) and \(\Wnz = \Enz + \Znz\) for the non-zonal parts of \(Z\) and \(W\), and \(\GammaE \equiv -\rmd\ln\Enz/\rmd t\) for the decay rate of the non-zonal electrostatic energy. For a structure of fixed shape, \(\GammaE = 2\gammaamp\), where \(\gammaamp\) is the decay rate of its amplitude.

\subsection{The strongly driven condensate}\label{sec:condensate}

At \(\eta \equiv L_n/L_T = 1\), the drive is above the fluid-interchange threshold \citep{MPH2018}, and the interchange grows at the same rate at every wavelength the box allows. A single \(k_y = 1\) streamer then collapses, in one burst, into \(k_x = 1\) zonal bands, viz., one jet in each direction across the periodic box, modulated by two box-scale vortices, one on each jet \citep{KennedyPlunkLetter}. The two vortices move in opposite directions, related by the symmetry of the pair-plasma equations under exchange of the species together with \(\phi\to-\phi\) and \(y\to-y\), and their relative phase sets the tilt of the flow between them, so the exchange \cref{eq:exchange} runs at \(\omb = 2|\omega_\text{adv}|\), twice the advection frequency of either vortex. Over a breathing cycle, the state changes little; over many cycles, the vortices decay. In what follows, we call \(k_y = 1\) the vortex and \(k_y = 0\) the jet, and the question of this paper is why the former decays while the latter does not.

\subsection{The reduced-box simulation and its restarts}\label{sec:runs}

All the data in this paper comes from one nonlinear simulation with the gyrokinetic code \gene{} \citep{Jenko2000} in the dipole flux tube of \citet{KennedyPlunkLetter}, at \(\eta = 1\), started from noise, in a box a quarter of the production box in each perpendicular direction, together with restarts of it. Its parameters are collected in \cref{tab:runs}. There is no collision operator: all irreversible removal is by fourth-order hyperdiffusion, spectral in \(x\) and \(y\) [rate \(\nu_\perp(\dx\,k_x/2)^4\), where \(\dx\) is the radial grid spacing, and likewise in \(y\)], and by finite-difference hyperdiffusion along the field line and in \(v_\parallel\). One property of the grid will matter in \cref{sec:erosion}: the radial grid spacing is comparable with the Debye length, \(\lambdaD\approx1.2\,\dx\). The run is continued for many thousands of breathing periods after the burst, until long after the vortices have disappeared. Times are in \(L_\text{ref}/c_\text{ref}\), lengths in \(\rho_\text{ref}\), and energies in the units of the \gene{} free-energy diagnostics \citep{BanonNavarro2011, BanonNavarro2016}.

\section{Theory of the vortices}\label{sec:theory}
% ===========================================================================

In this section, we derive the three elements of the picture of \cref{fig:cartoon}, viz., the instability of the jets, its saturation and the kinetic loss, and then put them together.

\subsection{The fluid limit}\label{sec:euler}

At the scale of the box, the jets and the vortices are flute-like, and the magnetic drift is slow compared with their motion. In this limit, the kinetic problem collapses to a fluid one.

Let us take the charge moment \(\sum_a e_a\intv\) of \cref{eq:kinetic} with \(\phibar = \phi\). The bracket commutes with the velocity integral, the gradient drive cancels between the species because \(e_a\,(e_af_0/T)\,v_{*a}^T = -e_a\,\partial_\psi f_0\) sums to zero, and the drift term survives only through the even part \(h_p + h_e\), since \(e_a\vda\) is the same for both species. Using \cref{eq:poisson} to write the charge moment as \((2n_0e^2/T)(1-\lambdaD^2\nabla_\perp^2)\phi\), and \(\pbrainline{\phi}{\phi} = 0\), we find\footnote{Strictly speaking, \cref{eq:euler} holds as written in a flux tube whose metric is constant along the field line; in the dipole, \(\nabla_\perp^2\) depends on \(l\), and a flute-like \(\phi\) satisfies it only on average along the line. This is consistent with the approximate flute-like character of the long-wavelength states \citep{KennedyPlunkManuscript}. In the flux tube of the simulations, the average is weighted by the Jacobian, and its effect on \cref{eq:rayleigh} is to replace \(k^2\) in the Laplacian by \(k^2\langle g^{yy}\rangle/\langle g^{xx}\rangle\), where \(g^{xx}\) and \(g^{yy}\) are the metric coefficients; this correction is of order unity, and the stability of the jets depends on it (\cref{sec:unstable}).}
\begin{equation}
\pt\nabla_\perp^2\phi + \pbra{\phi}{\nabla_\perp^2\phi} = \frac{T}{2n_0e\lambdaD^2}\,\partial_\alpha\intv\bar v_{dp}\left(h_p + h_e\right),
\label{eq:euler}
\end{equation}
where \(\bar v_{dp}\) is the positron drift. The right-hand side is the effect of the magnetic drift, which is slow compared with the motion of the vortices relative to the flow of their jets; we neglect it from here on, and confirm in \cref{sec:dissipation} that it plays no part in their energy budget. (It does not rely on the transit average either: the charge moment of the kinetic equation with the streaming of particles along the field line retained gives the same equation on averaging along the line, and the streaming adds only the loss of \cref{sec:loss}.) What remains is the two-dimensional Euler equation for the streamfunction \(\phi\) and the vorticity \(\nabla_\perp^2\phi\): the Debye length drops out, the gradients drop out, as they do for a symmetric pair plasma in a straight field \citep{Helander2014}, and the system of jets and vortices is an incompressible inviscid flow. Two properties of this limit matter in what follows. Firstly, multiplying the Euler equation by \(-\phi\) and integrating over the box shows that it conserves \(\int|\grad_\perp\phi|^2/2\), which is the electrostatic energy \(E\) of a flute-like potential in units of \(n_0e^2\lambdaD^2/T\): the vortices and the jets can exchange electrostatic energy, by \cref{eq:exchange}, but neither can lose it. Secondly, nothing in it refers to the amplitude of the gradients that drive the turbulence in which the condensate was born: the vortices are a property of the jets.

\subsection{The instability of the jets}\label{sec:rayleigh}

Let \(U \equiv \partial_\psi\Phi\) be the velocity of a zonal flow with potential \(\Phi(\psi)\).\footnote{Not to be confused with the specific flux-tube volume \(U = \oint\rmd l/B\) of \citet{KennedyPlunkLetter}, which does not appear in this paper.} Linearising the Euler equation about it, with \(\phi = \Phi + \varphi(\psi)\,\rme^{\rmi k\alpha - \rmi\omega t}\), where real parts are understood, and writing \(c \equiv \omega/k\) for the phase speed, gives Rayleigh's equation \citep{Rayleigh1880, DrazinReid2004},
\begin{equation}
\left(U - c\right)\left(\partial_\psi^2 - k^2\right)\varphi - U''\varphi = 0,
\label{eq:rayleigh}
\end{equation}
in which \(U''\) is the gradient of the zonal vorticity.\footnote{In the flux tube of the simulations, \(k^2\) in the Laplacian of \cref{eq:rayleigh} is replaced by \(k^2\langle g^{yy}\rangle/\langle g^{xx}\rangle\), where the angle brackets denote the average along the field line of the metric coefficients (\cref{sec:loss}).} The surfaces on which \(U = c\) are the critical layers of the perturbation: there, the perturbation is at rest in the frame of the fluid, \cref{eq:rayleigh} is singular, and the perturbation exchanges energy with the mean flow. For a perturbation whose growth rate \(\gamma_\text{R}\) is small compared with its frequency, the exchange at the critical layers determines the growth rate \citep{Briggs1970},
\begin{equation}
\gamma_\text{R} = \pi\,\frac{\sum_c U''_c\,|\varphi_c|^2/|U'_c|}{\partial_{\omega_r}\PV\!\int\rmd\psi\,k\,U''|\varphi|^2/(\omega_r - kU)},
\label{eq:gamma_rayleigh}
\end{equation}
where \(k > 0\), the sum runs over the critical layers, the subscript \(c\) denotes evaluation there, \(\omega_r\) is the real part of the frequency, and \(\PV\) denotes the principal value. Whether the perturbation takes energy from the mean flow and grows, or gives energy to it and decays, thus depends on the gradient of the zonal vorticity at its critical layers. This is the content of Rayleigh's theorem: a flow can be unstable only if \(U''\) changes sign. The derivation of \cref{eq:rayleigh,eq:gamma_rayleigh} and the proof of the theorem are standard \citep{DrazinReid2004}.

Two cases concern us. A jet that consists of a single harmonic of the box is stable to perturbations whose effective wavenumber along the flow exceeds the wavenumber of the jet, which is the case for all perturbations in the box of the simulations. A jet of the same amplitude whose shear layers are corrugated, so that each is split into strips of vorticity and \(U''\) changes sign close to the critical layers, is unstable. The first is the zonal flow of the condensate as it would be without the vortices; the second is the zonal flow that the simulations have (\cref{sec:unstable}).

\subsection{Saturation by trapping}\label{sec:pendulum}

A growing vortex changes the trajectories of the fluid at its critical layers, and this is what stops its growth. We use local coordinates \((x, y)\), radial and binormal, and the \exb{} streamfunction \(\Psi\).\footnote{In \gene{} units, \(\Psi = \phi/\Cxy\) with \(\Cxy\) the metric factor of the flux tube, \(x\) is in \(\rho_\text{ref}\) and \(t\) in \(L_\text{ref}/c_\text{ref}\). \gene{} writes the real field as \(\phi = \phi_0 + 2\operatorname{Re}(\phi_{k_y}\rme^{\rmi k_yy})\), so the cosine amplitude of a single \(k_y\) is \(A = 2|\phi_{k_y}|/\Cxy\).} In the frame \(\xi = y - ct\) that moves with the vortex, the flow is steady, and the trajectories are the level lines of the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
\dot x = -\partial_\xi\Ham,
\qquad
\dot\xi = \partial_x\Ham,
\qquad
\Ham = \Psi_\zon(x) + \Psi_v(x,\xi) - cx,
\label{eq:hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where \(\Psi_\zon\) and \(\Psi_v\) are the streamfunctions of the jet and of the vortex. Let \(x_c\) be a critical layer, \(U(x_c) = c\), with shear \(S \equiv U'(x_c)\), and let \(A\) be the amplitude of the vortex there. Expanding \cref{eq:hamiltonian} about \(x_c\) gives the Hamiltonian of a pendulum,
\begin{equation}
\Ham = \text{const} + \frac{S}{2}X^2 - A\cos(k\xi) + \dots,
\qquad
X = x - x_c.
\label{eq:pendulum}
\end{equation}
The fluid within a distance \(w\) of the critical layer is trapped: it circulates on closed streamlines, the `cat's eye' of Kelvin, at the frequency \(\omtr\), where
\begin{equation}
\omtr = |k|\sqrt{|S|A},
\qquad
w = 2\sqrt{\frac{A}{|S|}},
\label{eq:trapping}
\end{equation}
are the frequency of small oscillations about the centre of the eye and the half-width of its separatrix \citep{ONeil1965}; both scale as \(A^{1/2}\). On the two flanks of a jet, the shear has opposite signs, and the eyes are staggered by half a wavelength (see \cref{fig:cartoon}).

The growth rate \cref{eq:gamma_rayleigh} assumes that the fluid crosses the critical layer on its unperturbed trajectory. Once the fluid there has been trapped and has turned over, i.e., after a time of order \(1/\omtr\), the vorticity inside the cat's eye has been rearranged and the exchange with the mean flow ceases \citep{ONeil1965, Stewartson1978, WarnWarn1978}. An instability that grows at the rate \(\gamma_\text{R}\) therefore saturates when the trapping frequency has become a fixed multiple of the growth rate,
\begin{equation}
\omtr = C\,\gamma_\text{R},
\qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad
A = \frac{C^2\gamma_\text{R}^2}{k^2|S|},
\label{eq:saturation}
\end{equation}
where \(C\) is a pure number, which this argument does not determine. A vortex that has grown in this way has shaped its jets: at its critical layers, the fluid that it has trapped has been mixed, and the gradient of the zonal vorticity there has been flattened. The vortex thus helps to maintain the corrugation that makes its jets unstable; the two are parts of one state, and \cref{eq:saturation} is the relation between them. If the state changes slowly compared with \(\omtr\) and \(\gamma_\text{R}\), the relation is maintained: as the jets lose their instability, the vortices decay, and they disappear when the jets become stable.

\subsection{The kinetic loss}\label{sec:loss}

A flute-like vortex in the fluid limit conserves its energy. The vortices are damped because they are not quite flute-like, and the calculation of the damping has four steps: the motion of a particle along the field line, its response to a potential that varies along the line, the part of the vortex potential that does so, and the work that this part does.

To lowest order, a particle slides along its field line with \(\varepsilon\) and \(\lambda\) conserved, at \(v_\parallel = \pm v\sqrt{1-\lambda B(l)}\). Passing particles go round the periodic flux tube and trapped ones bounce; either way the motion is periodic, with the orbit frequency \(\omega_\tau = 2\pi/\tau\), where \(\tau = \hat\tau/v\) or \(2\hat\tau/v\) with \(\hat\tau\) of \cref{eq:orbit}. Since \(\omega_\tau\propto v\), every orbit frequency occurs, and for a thermal particle it is of the order of the transit frequency. With the orbit angle \(\theta = \omega_\tau t\), the potential that a particle sees is a periodic function of \(\theta\), \(\varphi(l(\theta)) = \sum_n\varphi_n(\lambda)\,\rme^{\rmi n\theta}\); the harmonic \(n = 0\) is the orbit average \(\phibar\), and for a flute-like potential all the others vanish.

Let the vortex be \(\phi = \varphi(x,l)\,\rme^{\rmi ky-\rmi\omega t}\). The zonal flow carries the particles past it, so at the radius \(x\) they see it oscillate at the Doppler-shifted frequency \(\wt(x) = \omega - kU(x)\), which vanishes at the critical layers. Following a particle along its orbit, without the slow drift and with the gradient terms, which cancel between the species, the non-adiabatic part of its distribution obeys \((\rmd/\rmd t - \rmi\wt)g_a = -\rmi\wt(e_af_0/T)\varphi(l(t))\), with \(\rmd/\rmd t = \omega_\tau\partial_\theta\). Each harmonic is solved separately,
\begin{equation}
g_a = \frac{e_af_0}{T}\sum_n\frac{\wt}{\wt - n\omega_\tau + \rmi0}\,\varphi_n\,\rme^{\rmi n\theta},
\label{eq:Hresponse}
\end{equation}
where causality, i.e., a response built up from the past, gives \(\omega\) a small positive imaginary part, and \(1/(X + \rmi0) = \PV(1/X) - \rmi\pi\delta(X)\). The term \(n = 0\) is all that the transit-averaged equations of \cref{sec:equations} retain. The others are resonant: the particles that complete \(n\) orbits in one period of the vortex as they see it, \(n\omega_\tau = \wt\), respond a quarter of a period out of phase and absorb energy. This is Landau damping \citep{Landau1946}, with the orbit frequency in place of \(k_\parallel v_\parallel\). Integrated over velocities, \cref{eq:Hresponse} gives the charge \((e/T)\rho[\varphi]\) on the right-hand side of \cref{eq:poisson}; for a flute-like potential \(\rho[\varphi] = \varphi\), a Boltzmann response that exchanges no energy.

A vortex cannot be exactly flute-like, because the left-hand side of \cref{eq:poisson} is not: in the flux tube, \(\nabla_\perp^2 = g^{xx}(l)\partial_x^2 + g^{yy}(l)\partial_y^2\), and the two metric coefficients do not vary in proportion along the line. Write \(\varphi = \varphi_0(x) + \varphi_1(x,l)\), with \(\varphi_0\) the volume-weighted average along the line and \(\varphi_1\) small. The average of the charge, \(-\lambdaD^2(\langle g^{xx}\rangle\partial_x^2 + \langle g^{yy}\rangle\partial_y^2)\phi\), is carried with the flow; this is the Euler equation. Its part that varies along the line is carried with the same flow, and since the average is conserved, \(\partial_x^2\phi\) changes following the flow at \(-\langle g^{yy}\rangle/\langle g^{xx}\rangle\) times the rate of \(\partial_y^2\phi\), which for the vortex is \(\rmi\wt k^2\varphi_0\). The varying charge therefore changes at the rate \(\rmi\wt\,k^2\lambdaD^2 s(l)\,\varphi_0\), with
\begin{equation}
s(l) \equiv \langle g^{yy}\rangle\left[\frac{g^{xx}(l)}{\langle g^{xx}\rangle} - \frac{g^{yy}(l)}{\langle g^{yy}\rangle}\right].
\label{eq:Hsource}
\end{equation}
Such a charge can change only through currents along the line, driven by \(\varphi_1\), whose induced charge \(\rho[\varphi_1] - \varphi_1\) changes at \(-\rmi\wt(\rho[\varphi_1] - \varphi_1)\). Equating the two rates gives \(\varphi_1 - \rho[\varphi_1] = k^2\lambdaD^2 s\,\varphi_0\), i.e.,
\begin{equation}
\varphi_1 = k^2\lambdaD^2\,\chi(l;\wt)\,\varphi_0,
\qquad
\chi - \rho[\chi] = s(l).
\label{eq:Hchi}
\end{equation}
The non-flute part is forced by the flute-like part through the geometry, is of relative size \(k^2\lambdaD^2\), and is linear in the amplitude. The equation for \(\chi\) is linear, along the field line, with the ratio of \(\wt\) to the transit frequency as its only parameter; we solve it by expanding \(\chi\) in Fourier harmonics along the line, with the orbits and their harmonics computed in the magnetic field of the flux tube. Because of the resonant term, \(\chi\) is complex: \(\varphi_1\) lags behind its forcing.

Only \(\varphi_1\) has a parallel electric field, and only the resonant particles absorb its work on average. Multiplying the field equation for \(\varphi_1\) by \(\varphi_1^*\), averaging along the line and taking the imaginary part, the power that the two species absorb per unit volume is \(P = (n_0e^2/T)\,k^4\lambdaD^4|\varphi_0|^2\,\Dfn(\wt)\), where
\begin{equation}
\Dfn(\wt) \equiv -\wt\left\langle s(l)\operatorname{Im}\chi(l;\wt)\right\rangle \geq 0
\label{eq:Hdissipation}
\end{equation}
is a function of the Doppler-shifted frequency that the geometry fixes once and for all [see \cref{fig:landau}(a)]. It vanishes as \(\wt\to0\), where the transit-averaged description applies, peaks where \(\wt\) is a few times the transit frequency, and falls at large \(\wt\), where few particles are fast enough to resonate. Integrating over the radius and dividing by the electrostatic energy of the vortex gives the loss rate
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\Enz} = 2\,k^2\lambdaD^2\,
\frac{\displaystyle k^2\int\rmd x\,|\varphi_0|^2\,\Dfn\!\left(\omega - kU\right)}
{\displaystyle\int\rmd x\left(\langle g^{xx}\rangle|\partial_x\varphi_0|^2 + \langle g^{yy}\rangle k^2|\varphi_0|^2\right)},
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
summed over the two vortices in the numerator and in the denominator. It converts the electrostatic energy of the vortices into entropy, which the sinks then remove, at a rate that does not depend on the sinks.

Equation \cref{eq:loss} has no adjustable parameter, and it makes three statements that can be tested. Firstly, the loss rate is independent of the amplitude of the vortices. Secondly, it is of the order of \(k^2\lambdaD^2\) times the transit frequency, so the vortices of a larger box are damped more slowly; at a fixed box it depends on the Debye length both through this factor and through the frequency of the vortices, which enters \(\Dfn(\wt)\). Thirdly, the lag of the non-flute potential, measured at any radius, on either vortex and at any time, is a single function of the local value of \(\wt\).

\subsection{The momentum of the damping}\label{sec:momentum}

The particles that absorb energy from a vortex also absorb its momentum, and they hand it to the jets. In the frame that moves with the vortex at its phase speed \(\omega/k\), its potential is steady and the energy of a particle is conserved, so a particle that gains energy \(\Delta\varepsilon\) and binormal momentum \(\Delta p_y\) in the frame of the box has \(\Delta\varepsilon = (\omega/k)\Delta p_y\). In the frame of the zonal flow it gains \((\wt/k)\Delta p_y\), which is the absorbed power \(P\); hence the particles at the radius \(x\) take binormal momentum from the vortex at the rate
\begin{equation}
f(x) = \frac{k\,P(x)}{\wt(x)}
\label{eq:Iforce}
\end{equation}
per unit volume. In a strong magnetic field, the binormal momentum of a guiding centre is tied to its radius, \(p_y = q_aBx\), so the momentum displaces a particle by \(\Delta x = \Delta p_y/q_aB\). Electrons and positrons gain momentum in the same direction and are displaced in opposite directions, carrying the radial current \(J_x = f/B\), whatever their shares. Its divergence changes the polarisation charge of the zonal flow, i.e., its vorticity, so that
\begin{equation}
\pt U = f
\label{eq:Ijets}
\end{equation}
in the fluid units of \cref{sec:euler}. The damping thus conserves the momentum of the jets and the vortices together, and removes their energy at the rate \(\int P\,\rmd x\). In the frame of the box, the vortex loses energy at the rate \((\omega/\wt)P\): \(P\) to the particles and \(Uf\) to the jets. Where \(\wt\) and \(\omega\) have opposite signs, in the cores of the jets that move faster than the vortex, the same absorption gives the vortex energy, \(-(\omega/\wt)P > 0\), and takes it from the jets: the damping is not a friction.

\subsection{The decay}\label{sec:decay}

Let us now put these elements together. Since the Euler equation conserves the electrostatic energy, the energy \(\Enz\) of the vortices changes only by exchange with the jets and by the kinetic loss:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\rmd\Enz}{\rmd t} = \mathcal{T} - \mathcal{L},
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{T}\) is the transfer of electrostatic energy from the jets to the vortices, averaged over the breathing, and \(\mathcal{L}\) is the kinetic loss \cref{eq:loss}. The total electrostatic energy of jets and vortices falls only through the loss, so the jets pay for whatever part of it the vortices are resupplied. The transfer is not given by the Euler equation alone: the particles that absorb the energy of the vortices also absorb their momentum and deposit it in the jets (\cref{sec:momentum}). Where the supply falls short of the loss, the vortices drain; as the dissipation of the simulation erodes the corrugation of the jets, the supply fails and the vortices collapse.

The theory thus makes the following predictions, which we test in \cref{sec:data}: (i)~the vortices have the frequency and the radial structure of the unstable mode of Rayleigh's equation on their jets; (ii)~their trapping frequency is of the order of the growth rate of that mode; (iii)~a vortex that is reduced in amplitude on unchanged jets regrows while the corrugation it needs is still in place; (iv)~the vortices lose electrostatic energy to entropy through the streaming of particles along the field line, at the rate \cref{eq:loss}, which does not depend on their amplitude and is smaller in a larger box; (v)~the jets supply the vortices with energy while they are unstable, less as they become less unstable, and finally absorb energy from them; and (vi)~the slow drain of the vortices is physical, the uncompensated part of their kinetic loss, while the dissipation that erodes the corrugation decides when they collapse.

The streamlines of the moving-frame Hamiltonian \cref{eq:hamiltonian} show the cat's eyes directly, one on each flank of the jet, staggered by half a wavelength [see \cref{fig:geometry}(b)].

Two premises of \cref{sec:theory} can be checked at once. Firstly, at the wavenumber of the vortices, the drift frequency of a thermal particle\footnote{Here the magnetic drift is the one that the code applies, viz., the curvature and \(\grad B\) drift of its geometry, averaged along the field line.} is smaller than the frequency of the vortex relative to the flow of its jet by orders of magnitude, and it displaces the resonance of a thermal particle from the critical layer by a small fraction of a grid cell, whereas the cat's eyes are several grid cells wide. The drift is therefore negligible for the motion of the vortices. Secondly, the breathing modulates the jets by a relative amount of the order of \(\Enz/\Ezon\), which is small, so the jets may be treated as steady in \cref{eq:rayleigh}.

\subsection{The vortices are the unstable mode of their jets}\label{sec:unstable}

Rayleigh's equation \cref{eq:rayleigh}, with the field-line-averaged metric of the flux tube, has one unstable mode in each direction on the measured zonal flow. Its frequency follows that of the vortices throughout the run, while both change substantially, and its radial structure is that of the vortices [see \cref{fig:rayleigh}(b,c)]. This is prediction (i). The reason for the instability is visible in the zonal vorticity: each shear layer between the jets is split into two strips, and the strips sit on the critical layers of the two vortices, where the gradient of the zonal vorticity is flattened [see \cref{fig:rayleigh}(a)]. This is the mark that the cat's eyes leave on the jets, and it stays in place throughout the decay. A single harmonic of the same amplitude is stable; it is this corrugation, a few Debye lengths in scale, that makes the jets unstable. The growth rate falls slowly during the decay and vanishes at the collapse [see \cref{fig:rayleigh}(d)].

The corrugation is left by the burst, changes little while the vortices are present, and decays faster once they have gone: the vortices maintain it, and without them the perpendicular sink erodes it. \subsection{The vortices are saturated by trapping}\label{sec:saturated}

\Cref{fig:saturation} tests prediction (ii), the saturation law \cref{eq:saturation}, with \(\omtr\) measured from the vortices and \(\gamma_\text{R}\) computed from the jets. The law holds through the whole decay of the original run, including the collapse, with a single value of \(C\), while the energy of the vortices falls by orders of magnitude.

The amplitude tests show how the relation is restored when it is broken. Reducing the vortices leaves them below the saturated level of their jets, and they regrow, which is prediction (iii) [see \cref{fig:amplitude}(a)]. They do not regrow to their former size, however, because, as they regrow, they flatten the corrugation of their jets further and so lower \(\gamma_\text{R}\): the two approach each other. In the \(\epsilon = 0.3\) restart, they meet on the curve of the original run. In the \(\epsilon = 0.03\) restart, whose cat's eyes are no wider than a grid cell, the vortices stay below it [see \cref{fig:saturation}(b)].

The loss rate \(\mathcal{L}/\Enz\) is constant while the energy of the vortices falls by orders of magnitude, and it is the same in the amplitude tests as in the original run: the loss is linear in the energy of the vortices. Nearly all of it is carried by the streaming of particles along the field line, and the small remainder by the magnetic drift.

All three statements of \cref{sec:loss} about the kinetic loss hold (see \cref{fig:landau}). The measured lag follows the predicted \(\Dfn(\wt)\), with departures close to the critical layers, where \(\wt\) is small, and at the largest \(\wt\). The loss rate measured by the energy diagnostics of the code, in the channel of parallel streaming, is the predicted one throughout the decay of the reduced-box run, while the energy of the vortices falls by orders of magnitude; it is the same in the restarts with reduced amplitude; and in the production run of \citet{KennedyPlunkLetter}, whose box is four times larger in each perpendicular direction, it is smaller by the predicted factor. In runs from noise with the Debye length doubled and halved, \(\lambdaD^2\) changes by a factor of sixteen and the frequency of the vortices by a factor of eight; the measured loss rate changes by only a factor of three, and it is the predicted one in both, with no adjustable parameter. This is the remaining part of prediction (iv). The entropy that the loss produces is removed by the velocity-space hyperdiffusion; the perpendicular sink acts almost entirely on the zonal flow.

The exchange with the jets behaves as prediction (v) requires. Throughout the slow phase of the decay, the jets supply the vortices with electrostatic energy at a rate that makes up for most of the loss, so the slow decay of \cref{fig:history} is the small difference between two larger rates. As the vortices weaken, and their jets become less unstable, the supply fades, passes through zero, and becomes a drain: the vortices give energy back to their jets, and collapse [see \cref{fig:exchange}(b)]. The restarts follow the same curve: the \(\epsilon = 0.3\) restart has the loss rate of the original run and a smaller supply, and the \(\epsilon = 0.03\) restart has the loss rate of the original run and, once its regrowth is over, a drain. The acceleration of the decay, and its dependence on amplitude, are therefore those of the exchange with the jets, not of the damping. The mean transfer from the jets is also why the zonal flow decays faster while the vortices are large than after they have gone [see \cref{fig:history}(b)].

\subsection{What sets the rate of the decay}\label{sec:erosion}

Prediction (vi) is tested by the sink tests (see \cref{fig:sinktests}) and by a run repeated from noise with the radial hyperdiffusion switched off. Dividing the velocity-space hyperdiffusion by four changes neither the loss rate \(\mathcal{L}/\Enz\), as befits Landau damping, nor the decay. Multiplying the perpendicular hyperdiffusion by four brings on the collapse at once; dividing it by four, or reducing the radial hyperdiffusion further by doubling the radial resolution, slows the late decay and removes the collapse, although the vortices go on losing energy to entropy at the same rate. Without the radial hyperdiffusion, the vortices drain exactly as in the reference run during the slow phase, and drain more slowly only later, once the reference run begins to accelerate. The slow drain is therefore physical; the acceleration and the collapse of the reference run are set by the radial hyperdiffusion and the grid. Without the radial hyperdiffusion, the vortices do not collapse, and drain at a nearly constant rate, a small fraction of their loss rate. How the loss is shared between jets and vortices depends on the resolution of the Debye scale, which sets the structure at the critical layers.

The collapse itself is set by the grid. The fluid in the cat's eyes turns over many times while the vortices decay, so \cref{eq:saturation} is maintained; the decay speeds up as the eyes narrow, and the vortices collapse when the half-width \(w\) of the eyes has fallen to about one grid cell, which is also about one Debye length in this simulation, and the jets become stable [see \cref{fig:rayleigh}(d)].

\subsection{How the jets pay for the loss}\label{sec:payment}

The energy budget of the jets closes. In every run, the rate at which the jets lose energy is the part of the loss of the vortices that the vortices do not drain, plus the hyperdiffusion of the jets, with no adjustable constant [see \cref{fig:supply}(a)]. Two effects beyond the Euler equation carry this exchange, and both are measured directly from the zonal budget of the simulation.

First, the momentum of the damping (\cref{sec:momentum}). Computed from the loss formula, the force \cref{eq:Iforce} has the phase of the part of the zonal forcing that the Euler Reynolds stress does not explain, on the dominant harmonic of the jets, and it removes the energy that the jets lose beyond the Euler exchange.

Second, the Reynolds stress of the vortices, computed from their field-line-averaged potential, together with the deposited momentum, accounts for the nonlinear forcing of the dominant harmonic of the jets; at the fine harmonics of the jets, the Reynolds stress is the measured nonlinear forcing, and parallel streaming returns it. The two exchanges are each of the order of the power absorbed by the particles, and of opposite sign for the vortices: the Reynolds stress passes energy from the vortices to the jets, while the damping, through the momentum it deposits, passes energy from the jets to the vortices. The drain of the vortices is their small difference [see \cref{fig:balance}]. The near-cancellation follows from the conservation of momentum: the particles return the momentum they take from the vortices to the jets, and the Reynolds stress, which moves momentum between vortices and jets without loss, nearly restores it to the vortices; what changes the energy of the vortices is the mismatch between where the particles deposit the momentum and where the stress takes it from.

The jets lose energy nonlinearly at about the Landau loss rate of the vortices. Most of it reaches the vortices; the rest reaches their harmonics, the fine binormal structure of the cat's eyes, which are Landau damped as fast as they receive it, at the rate that the loss formula gives at their own wavenumber and Doppler-shifted frequency. The vortices lose a little more through the magnetic drift, and drain by what is left.

\section{Summary and discussion}\label{sec:summary}
% ===========================================================================

We have given a theory of the breathing vortices of the strongly driven dipole pair-plasma condensate and tested it against a reduced-box simulation and restarts of it.

Firstly, at the scale of the box, the jets and the vortices obey the two-dimensional Euler equation, and the vortices are the unstable mode of Rayleigh's equation \cref{eq:rayleigh} on their jets: it has their frequency, throughout the run, and their radial structure. The jets are unstable because their shear layers are corrugated, on a scale of a few Debye lengths, and the instability saturates by trapping.

Secondly, the vortices are damped kinetically and linearly. The field equation forces on them a small part that varies along the field line; this part is Landau damped by the particles that stream along the line; and the resulting loss rate \cref{eq:loss}, which has no adjustable parameter and does not depend on the amplitude of the vortices, is the measured one, in the reduced box, in the restarts, in the larger box of the production run, and in runs with the Debye length doubled and halved.

Thirdly, the slow drain of the vortices is physical: it is the same with the radial hyperdiffusion switched off, and without it the vortices do not collapse. The jets pay for most of the loss, and the energy budget of the jets closes with no adjustable constant. Beyond the Euler equation, the damped vortices deposit their momentum in the jets through the resonant particles; with the Reynolds stress, this accounts for the forcing of the dominant harmonic of the jets. The two exchanges nearly cancel for the vortices, whose drain is their small difference.

Fourthly, the late acceleration of the decay and the collapse of the vortices in the reference run are set by the radial hyperdiffusion and by the resolution of the Debye scale: they follow the perpendicular sink, not the velocity-space one.

In a real plasma, the corrugation would be eroded by collisions.
